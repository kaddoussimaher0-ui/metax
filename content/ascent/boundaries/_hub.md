# The layer where the Ascent states what it refuses to claim

Boundaries is the Ascent's honesty layer — the group that states, in advance, what the pillar will not assert, where silicon's limits actually are, and how ready each rung is. It exists because a research-preview pillar owes its reader a map of its own edges more than a map of its confident middle. This group holds the limits of silicon, an explicit account of what the Ascent is not, the banned-claims register, the bridge passport that gates entry, and the rung-status table that dates each rung's readiness. It is the most important group in the pillar, because it is where the Ascent tells you what to distrust.

## Why a research pillar leads with its boundaries

A pillar reading a live frontier can mislead in two ways: by overclaiming what is settled, and by hiding what it cannot summarise. Boundaries prevents both. The banned-claims register names the specific overclaims the estate refuses — most famously the DishBrain-style like-for-like efficiency comparisons that are category errors. The rung-status table dates each rung's readiness so a reader never mistakes an outline for a finished summary. And the "what Ascent is not" page states the pillar's negative space directly. Leading with boundaries is the estate's register-closure discipline applied to itself: an enumeration of what is claimed invites the inference that the unnamed is permitted, so the pillar states its floor explicitly.

## The map — what lives in this group

**The Limits of Silicon** states where the substrate actually reaches its edge, distinguishing established limits from speculative ones. **What Ascent Is Not** states the pillar's negative space — not a claim that biology beats silicon today, not human-cell research, not a marketing story. **Banned Claims** lists the specific overclaims the estate refuses, with reasons. **Bridge Passport & Gating** states how a learner earns entry from TopTech's Branch D gateway. **Rung Status Table** dates each rung's readiness under the publication-readiness protocol.

## The worked instance — the banned-claims register

The banned-claims register is the group's sharpest instance of the estate refusing to overclaim. Its clearest entry bans the DishBrain-vs-DeepMind style "28,800×" efficiency comparison as a category error: comparing a biological substrate's energy use to a silicon model's on a single benchmark treats two incommensurable things as commensurable, and the estate forbids the comparison even though it would make a compelling headline. Banning the claim in advance is the estate binding itself before a result can tempt it — the same move the Method notation group makes with its red lines.

## Limits of this group

Boundaries states limits; it cannot make the frontier less uncertain. The banned-claims register lists the overclaims the estate has anticipated, not all possible ones, which is why the residual test and unnamed-harm discipline run alongside. And the rung-status table reflects the current build, which on a research preview means most rungs are outlines rather than finished summaries — the table's honesty is the point.

## The falsifier for this group

The group's governing claim is that leading a research pillar with its boundaries serves a reader better than leading with its confident claims. The refutation: if readers shown the banned-claims register and status table judge the frontier no more accurately than readers shown only the rungs, the boundary layer adds caution without calibration.

## What this group obliges

Read "what Ascent is not" and the banned-claims register before the rungs; they tell you what the pillar refuses to pretend to know. Check a rung's entry in the status table before you trust it as a finished summary. And carry the banned claims forward: the estate forbids them for you as much as for itself, because a category error laundered into a headline is a harm whoever commits it.
