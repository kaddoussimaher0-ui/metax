Decay-awareness is easy to preach and hard to practise. So here is a real one: an operator ran the pre-registered playbook, hit their kill criteria on one bet, and shipped the honest post-mortem instead of quietly deleting the evidence. This is what the discipline looks like when it stops being a slide and starts costing something.

## The setup

The operator committed to a 90-day capstone with three bets. Before any work began — this ordering is the whole point — each bet was written up with:

- A **pre-registered hypothesis** and an explicit null, stating what would count as the idea being wrong.
- **Kill criteria** — the specific, measurable conditions under which the bet would be stopped, decided in advance and in writing.
- **Unit economics** defined up front: what each outcome could cost and still be worth pursuing.

Writing the kill criteria first is deceptively hard. In the calm before a launch it is easy to say "I'll stop if it isn't working." Under the sunk-cost pressure of week six, "isn't working" becomes infinitely negotiable — unless you fixed the number before you were emotionally invested. That is exactly why the rule is pre-registered.

## What happened

Two of the three bets progressed. They were not runaway successes, but their leading indicators crossed the pre-set thresholds and the unit economics held, so they continued into deeper validation.

The third bet hit its kill criteria at **day 41.** The leading indicator the operator had nominated as the early signal never crossed the pre-set line. There was the usual temptation — a plausible story about why next month would be different, a feature that "just needed one more iteration," a cohort that "wasn't representative." All of it was the sound of a dead hypothesis asking for more money.

Per the rule, the operator killed it. No sunk-cost rationalisation. No "let's give it another month." The bet was stopped on the day the criteria said to stop it, and the budget that would have chased it was returned to the two bets that were actually working.

## The post-mortem

The honest write-up is the real artifact of the capstone — worth more than any vanity case study. It covered four things, plainly:

1. **What was assumed** — the model of the world the bet depended on.
2. **What the null was** — and therefore what would have proven the assumption wrong.
3. **What the data actually showed** — including the leading indicator that never moved.
4. **What changed in the operator's model afterward** — the updated belief, so the failure produced knowledge rather than just a loss.

That fourth point is what separates a post-mortem from an obituary. A bet that dies without changing your model has taught you nothing; a bet that dies and updates your map has paid for itself in judgment.

## Why publish it

The instinct in most of the industry is to bury losing bets and publish only the wins, which produces a highlight reel that quietly teaches everyone the wrong lesson: that good operators do not fail. They do. The difference is that good operators fail **on schedule, on purpose, and in public**, so the loss becomes a shared asset rather than a private embarrassment.

> Killing a bet on schedule is not failure. Ignoring your own kill criteria is.

## The rails at work

This one small story exercises three of the six companion rails that run through every series in the stack:

- **Falsifiability** — a null written before the result, so the bet could actually be wrong.
- **Kill criteria** — a stopping rule fixed in advance and honoured under pressure.
- **Unit economics** — an outcome priced up front, so "working" had a definition beyond a feeling.
