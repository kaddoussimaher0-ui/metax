# What the relational stratum injures

Stratum II reaches the person not as a mind but as a party in relation — to a service, to a watcher, to a system that decides about them. Where Stratum I injures the faculty of reasoning, Stratum II injures the conditions under which a person can be dealt with fairly: knowing what is being done to them, retaining a zone of practical obscurity, and being able to contest a decision that affects them. The stratum is grounded in *Gharar* — structurally concealed uncertainty in a dealing — and in *Tajassus*, the prying into what a person has not disclosed, and in *Adl*, the right to be heard.

# HX-04 — The Human as Product

The thesis is that when a service is funded by selling access to its users rather than by the users, the user becomes the good transacted, and the terms of that transaction are structurally concealed from the one party who is its subject. The mechanism is *Gharar*: the user cannot know what is collected, inferred, or sold about them, so they cannot price the exchange they are in. The evidence is the documented economics of advertising-funded platforms. The strongest counter is that ad funding democratises access by making services free at the point of use — a real benefit, answered by observing that the harm is the concealment of the terms, not the funding model as such.

HX-04 is one of the seven absolutes, at §H.3(a)(ii), bounded to the case where a person is made the product without a disclosed, priceable account of the exchange. Its safe harbour is full disclosure of what is collected, inferred, and sold. Its falsifier: evidence that users of ad-funded services can, in fact, accurately price the exchange would retire it. **This is the second of the two harms MetaX's own statement marks unremedied,** for the same ad-slot reason as HX-01.

# HX-05 — Ambient Surveillance and the End of Practical Obscurity

The thesis is that pervasive collection removes the practical obscurity — the friction that once protected most of life from most observation — that a free person depends on, and that the removal is a structural property of always-on instrumentation rather than a choice to spy. The mechanism is that collection is cheap, retention is default, and inference reconstructs what was never disclosed. The evidence draws on the data-broker and re-identification literature. The strongest counter is that surveillance also enables safety and service — answered by narrowing the duty to minimisation and to a maintained zone of non-collection, not to a ban on collection.

HX-05 is not absolute; it generates a minimisation duty on the Operator. Its safe harbour is data minimisation with published retention limits. Its falsifier: evidence that ambient collection does not enable re-identification or inference beyond what was disclosed would retire it.

# HX-06 — Algorithmic Governance Without Due Process

The thesis is that when a system decides about a person — eligibility, ranking, suspension — without a stated reason, a route of appeal, or a human answerable for the decision, it exercises governance while removing the due process that governance owes. This is Lessig's "code is law" in its most acute form: a regulatory substitution in which the regulated party has no standing. The mechanism is that the decision is opaque, unappealable, and diffused across a system no single party will own. The evidence includes documented automated-decision failures in benefits and moderation. The strongest counter is that human decisions are also biased and opaque — answered by observing that a human decision at least has an author who can be asked.

HX-06 is one of the seven absolutes, at §H.3(a)(iii), bounded to consequential decisions made without any reason, appeal, or answerable party. Its safe harbour is a stated reason, a route of appeal, and a named decider. Its falsifier: evidence that unexplained automated decisions produce outcomes no less fair and no less contestable than reasoned ones would retire it.

# The mechanism these three share

All three relational harms remove a condition of fair dealing rather than injuring the person directly: HX-04 removes priceability, HX-05 removes obscurity, HX-06 removes contestability. Each is a harm of asymmetry — one party knows and decides, the other cannot know and cannot contest — which is why the stratum's remedies are all disclosure and process rather than restriction.

# The worked instance — the estate's minimisation posture

The estate applies this stratum to itself with a mixed result. It carries no third-party analytics and stores no learner behavioural data, which discharges HX-05's minimisation duty structurally. Its credential and appeals machinery — MX-016, under which appeals are decided by someone other than the party appealed against — discharges HX-06's process duty. But HX-04 remains unremedied for as long as the ad slots imply an advertising relationship, and the estate's statement says so rather than claiming the minimisation posture cures it.

# Limits of this stratum

This stratum reaches dealing, not the market's structure (Stratum III) or the material world (Stratum IV). Its absolutes bind only within their bounded elements: a collection that is minimised and disclosed is outside HX-05 regardless of volume, and a decision with a reason and an appeal is outside HX-06 regardless of automation.

# The falsifier for the stratum

The stratum's governing claim is that concealed terms, ambient collection, and unappealable automated decision structurally injure fair dealing. The refutation: if persons in these relations can in fact price the exchange, retain obscurity, and contest decisions at rates indistinguishable from persons in transparent relations, the stratum's premise fails.

# What this stratum obliges

If you fund a service by selling its users, you owe a disclosed, priceable account under HX-04 — and if you cannot give one, you owe an unremedied mark, as the estate does. If you collect, you owe minimisation and retention limits under HX-05. If you decide about people, you owe a reason, an appeal, and a named decider under HX-06.
