# The grading system that gives every claim its standing

Epistemics is the group that grades knowledge. Every substantive claim in the estate carries an evidence class, a decay interval, and a falsifier — and this group defines what those are and how they are assigned. It exists because the difference between an academy and a blog is that an academy states the standing of its claims: how sure it is, how it could be wrong, and when the claim expires. Its six pages fix the evidence classes E0–E4, the decay standard, the grammar a falsifier must follow, the counterproductivity test, the register of banned claims, and the register of open problems.

## Why knowledge must be graded, not merely stated

A claim asserted without its standing invites the reader to treat a guess and a replicated finding as equal, which is the failure most content makes. The evidence classes prevent it: E0 is the estate's own position, marked as such; E1 is a single source or a frontier reading; E2 is observational evidence, dated; E3 is a well-established synthesis; E4 is a replicated, citable finding. A reader can see, on the face of any claim, which class it carries, and can therefore calibrate trust rather than extend it uniformly. The decay interval does the same across time: a claim cannot outlive its evidence unnoticed, because its review date is stated.

## The map — what lives in this group

**Evidence Classes (E0–E4)** defines the five-level grading and how a class is assigned. **The Decay Standard** attaches a review interval to each class and states what happens when it elapses. **Falsifier Grammar** defines what a well-formed falsifier must contain — a named observation, an evidence class, and where possible a date. **The Counterproductivity Test** is the Illich-derived test that converts "is this good?" into "has this deployment crossed its threshold, and how would we know?" **The Banned Claim Register** lists claims the estate refuses to make, with reasons. **The Open Problems Register** lists the questions the estate has not resolved.

## How a claim moves through the epistemic machinery

A claim enters with an evidence class and a decay interval. It is stated with a falsifier that follows the grammar — a named observation that would show it wrong. It is checked against the banned-claim register to ensure the estate is not asserting something it has ruled out. And when its decay interval elapses, or a relevant change lands, it is re-examined: if it fails its falsifier, it moves to the corrections record; if the question is genuinely unresolved, it moves to the open-problems register rather than being asserted either way.

## The worked instance — the counterproductivity test

The counterproductivity test is the group's most operational instance. Illich's thesis is that every tool passes, at some scale, a second watershed beyond which it frustrates the end it was built to serve. The test makes this answerable: a deployer states, before deployment, the outcome the system exists to serve, the measure of that outcome, and the level at which they would concede the watershed has been crossed. That is a pre-registration, and the estate's pre-registration desk is the instrument that seals it. The test converts an unanswerable value judgement into a dated, measurable commitment.

## Limits of this group

Epistemics grades claims; it cannot make a claim true, only state how sure the estate is and how it could be wrong. The evidence classes are the estate's own judgement of standing, not an external certification. And the open-problems register is, by construction, a list of things the estate cannot yet answer — its length is a measure of honesty, not of failure.

## The falsifier for this group

The group's governing claim is that graded, dated, falsifiable claims carry more usable information than ungraded assertions. The refutation: if readers given evidence classes and decay dates calibrate their trust no better than readers given bare assertions, the grading adds ceremony without signal.

## What this group obliges

Read the evidence class and decay date on any claim before you rely on it; they tell you how much weight it bears. State a falsifier for every substantive claim you make, following the grammar. And if you found an academy, adopt the evidence classes and the decay standard — a credential's value rests on the standing of the claims behind it, which this group is what defines.
