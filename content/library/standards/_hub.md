# The disciplines every page in the estate is held to

Standards is the group that holds the estate's published disciplines — the style, symbol, decay, falsifiability, accessibility, and bilingual standards that every page is measured against, plus the master generation prompt. It exists because a standard that is not written down cannot be checked, and an estate that claims to be consistent, accessible, and decay-aware must publish the standards those claims rest on. Every standard here is a page other pages cite by identifier: when a course claims a technique decays in twelve months, it is applying the decay standard published in this group. Its seven pages are the style guide, symbol standard, decay standard, falsifiability standard, accessibility standard, bilingual standard, and master generation prompt.

## Why the standards are published, not internal

An internal standard protects the author; a published standard protects the reader. The estate publishes its standards so a reader can follow a citation from any page to the exact discipline it claims to meet, and check whether it does. This is the estate's method applied to its own consistency: the decay standard defines what "decays" means so a claim's expiry is not arbitrary; the accessibility standard states the conformance target and publishes the gaps; the bilingual standard fixes which language is authoritative for what. Each is a reference other pages depend on.

## The map — what lives in this group

**Publishing Style Guide** fixes how the estate writes — declarative, no marketing verbs, prose over bullets. **Symbol Standard** fixes the glyphs and their meanings. **Decay Standard** defines the decay classes and review intervals. **Falsifiability Standard** defines what a well-formed falsifier must carry. **Accessibility Standard** states the WCAG target and publishes the gaps as defects. **Bilingual Standard** fixes the language-authority rule. **Master Generation Prompt** is the prompt that governs how the estate's pages are generated to standard.

## The worked instance — the bilingual standard

The bilingual standard is the group's clearest instance of a discipline answering a structural harm. It fixes which language is authoritative for what — Arabic for Meta-X, BCIA, and the Lexicon; English for the License — so that a translation can never become the route by which a term is redefined. This is the License's linguistic-flattening and semantic-capture harms answered by a published rule: because the authoritative language is fixed, an English translation of the Arabic layer books cannot become the citable text, and the estate cannot let translation flatten the tradition it draws from.

## Limits of this group

The standards document the disciplines; they cannot make a page comply, only state what compliance is and let it be checked. The accessibility standard publishes gaps, which means it admits the estate is not fully conformant — the honesty is the point, but the gaps are real. And the master generation prompt is a tool, not a guarantee: a page generated by it still must be checked against the other standards.

## The falsifier for this group

The group's governing claim is that published standards produce a more consistent, accessible, checkable estate than internal ones. The refutation: if the estate's pages diverge from its published standards no less than a comparable estate's diverge from internal ones, publishing adds transparency without improving consistency.

## What this group obliges

When a page cites a standard, follow the citation and check the page against it — that is what publishing the standard is for. If you rely on assistive technology, read the accessibility standard's published gaps. And if you found an academy, adopt these standards: the shared disciplines are what let the estate charter many academies without each becoming a separate quality risk.
