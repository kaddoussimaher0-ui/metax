# The checkable base under every claim in the estate

Sources & Data is the group that holds the estate's evidentiary and machine-readable base — the bibliography a claim can be traced to, the citation ledger that tracks whether a source still resolves, the open data anyone can download, and the schemas that define it. It exists because a claim is only checkable if its source is findable and still there, and data is only trustworthy if its shape is documented. This group is where the estate makes its evidence and its data open. Its four pages are the estate bibliography, the citation ledger, the open data downloads, and the schemas — the last two thin-by-nature and exempt from the word floor under DEF-2026-043.

## Why sources and data are published together

A citation that points at a dead link certifies nothing, and open data with no schema is a blob. This group answers both: the citation ledger tracks link rot and retraction so a citation's health is visible, and the schemas document the shape of the open data so it can actually be used. Publishing the data openly is also the anti-enclosure limb of the License's successor harm — the estate does not withdraw the material a successor would need to build an alternative, which is why the open-data downloads exist rather than being locked behind an API.

## The map — what lives in this group

**Estate Bibliography** lists the sources the estate's claims draw on. **Citation Ledger** tracks each citation's health — whether it still resolves, and whether the source has been retracted. **Open Data (JSON)** is the downloadable data behind the estate's counts and catalogues, published so anyone can audit or reuse it. **Schemas** documents the shape of that data — a thin-by-nature reference page.

## The worked instance — the citation ledger

The citation ledger is the group's clearest instance of the custody and successor harms answered in practice. Link rot is real: scholarly and judicial citations decay at measurable rates, and a platform-hosted source can vanish on shutdown. The ledger tracks each citation's status, so a reader can see whether a source still resolves and whether it has been retracted — and a rotted citation downgrades the claim it supports rather than being silently trusted. This is the estate refusing to let its evidence quietly evaporate, which a static list of references would allow.

## Limits of this group

Two pages here — the downloads and the schemas — are thin-by-nature and carry the DEF-2026-043 exemption, because padding a schema to twelve hundred words would be the busywork harm the License names. The bibliography is only as complete as the estate's sourcing, and the License's own open bibliography defect (DEF-2026-056) is a live example of a gap the estate refuses to fill with machine-generated references. And the citation ledger tracks health but cannot resurrect a source that has truly vanished.

## The falsifier for this group

The group's governing claim is that a tracked citation ledger and open, schema-documented data make the estate's evidence more durable and reusable than a static reference list. The refutation: if the estate's citations rot no less under the ledger, or its open data goes unused, the extra machinery adds cost without benefit.

## What this group obliges

Follow a claim to its source in the bibliography before you rely on it, and check the citation ledger for the source's health. Reuse the open data under its schema rather than re-deriving counts — it is published so you can audit and build on it. And treat the anti-enclosure commitment as real: the estate publishes the data a successor would need, which is the successor harm answered in practice.
