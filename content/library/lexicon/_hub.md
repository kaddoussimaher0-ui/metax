# The bilingual vocabulary that holds the estate's words steady

The Lexicon is the group that fixes the estate's vocabulary — every term it uses, in both English and Arabic, with the contested ones presented as arguments rather than settled definitions. It exists because an estate of hundreds of cross-linked pages cannot reason together if its words drift, and because the words of accountability are exactly the ones under the most pressure to drift. This group holds all entries, the Arabic root index, the contested terms, and the lexicon method. Lexicon entries are held to a lower word floor by design — an entry that runs to an essay has stopped being an entry — and they carry the thin-by-nature exemption under DEF-2026-043.

## Why the lexicon exists and is bilingual

A term with normative weight — "open," "secure," "consent" — drifts under commercial pressure, always toward the incumbent's existing conduct, which is the semantic-capture harm the License names. The lexicon holds such terms steady by publishing their definitions, and it is bilingual because the estate's Arabic material is authoritative for Meta-X and BCIA: a term must be held steady in both languages, or translation becomes a route to redefinition. The Arabic root index lets a reader trace a term to its root, which is how the estate keeps the Arabic tradition's own vocabulary rather than flattening it into English.

## The map — what lives in this group

**All Entries** is the full vocabulary, each entry short by design. **Root Index (Arabic)** organises terms by Arabic root. **Contested Terms** presents terms whose meaning is disputed, with competing definitions side by side. **Lexicon Method** states how an entry is written, dated, and given a decay class.

## The worked instance — the contested-terms section

The contested-terms section is the group's sharpest instance of the estate's method. For a word like "open" or "secure," it does not simply state the estate's definition; it presents the competing definitions side by side, dated, and only then states MetaX's working choice, marked as such. A reader is shown the argument and the estate's position within it, rather than being handed an answer. This is the semantic-capture harm answered directly: the estate refuses to let a loaded word's meaning be settled by whoever uses it most, and instead makes the definitional argument happen in the open.

## Limits of this group

The lexicon holds the estate's own vocabulary; it cannot compel the wider world to use its definitions. Entries are short by design and carry the thin-by-nature exemption, so they do not meet the estate's general word floor — padding them would make them essays, which is the busywork harm the License names. And the contested terms present arguments, not verdicts: the estate states its working choice but does not claim to have settled the dispute.

## The falsifier for this group

The group's governing claim is that publishing definitions, and presenting contested terms as arguments, holds the estate's words steadier than leaving them implicit. The refutation: if the estate's terms drift under use no less than an estate's without a lexicon, the vocabulary discipline adds pages without holding meaning.

## What this group obliges

When you meet a loaded term in the estate, check the lexicon for its working definition before you assume its meaning. Read the contested terms as arguments to weigh, not verdicts to accept. And under the bilingual standard, remember that for Meta-X, BCIA, and the Lexicon itself, the Arabic is authoritative — the English entry is a translation of equal standing, not the source.
