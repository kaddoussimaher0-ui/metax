# The evidence a claim of mastery must actually carry

Evidence & Proof is the group that defines what a learner must show to earn a credential — the artefacts, the dossier, and the proof standard. It exists because a credential is only as good as the evidence behind it, and a claim of mastery with no inspectable proof is indistinguishable from a boast. This group fixes the proof ladder P0–P3, the submission pack a learner assembles, the dossier templates, the artefact requirements, and the AI-disclosure rule under which a learner must state where a machine assisted their work. Its five pages are the proof ladder, the submission pack, the dossier and templates, the artefact requirements, and AI disclosure.

## Why proof is laddered

Not every credential demands the same weight of evidence, so the proof ladder grades it: P0 is a completed artefact, P1 adds independent verification of the artefact, P2 adds a demonstrated application under stated conditions, and P3 adds sustained, reviewed contribution. A rung on the mastery ladder maps to a proof level, so a learner knows exactly what evidence their target credential demands. This prevents both under-proving — issuing a credential on thin evidence — and over-demanding — requiring P3 proof for a Practitioner mark, which would be the over-demand the estate warns against.

## The map — what lives in this group

**The Proof Ladder (P0–P3)** grades the evidence a credential demands. **Submission Pack** is what a learner assembles and submits. **Dossier & Templates** provides the structure a submission takes. **Artefact Requirements** states what makes an artefact acceptable evidence. **AI Disclosure** requires a learner to state where a machine assisted, under MX-007 — not to forbid AI use, but to make it visible so the review can weigh it.

## The worked instance — the AI-disclosure rule

The AI-disclosure page is the group's clearest instance of the estate's honesty applied to learners. It does not ban AI assistance — that would be unenforceable and often counterproductive. Instead it requires disclosure: a learner states where a machine assisted their artefact, so the reviewer can weigh the evidence knowing what the learner did and what a tool did. This is the synthetic-provenance harm answered by disclosure rather than prohibition, applied to credentialing. An undisclosed AI-assisted artefact presented as unaided work is a false representation the rule exists to prevent.

## Limits of this group

The proof ladder grades evidence, not truth — a well-documented artefact can still demonstrate a shallow competence, which is why review by a human applies the rubric on top. The AI-disclosure rule depends on learner honesty, which the estate cannot fully verify, only require and check where it can. And the artefact requirements are demanding, which means earning a credential is real work by design.

## The falsifier for this group

The group's governing claim is that laddered, disclosed, artefact-based proof produces more meaningful credentials than a test score. The refutation: if credentials earned through this evidence process certify competence no more reliably than credentials earned by examination alone, the dossier machinery adds cost without signal.

## What this group obliges

Assemble the proof level your target credential demands — no less, and no more than the ladder requires. Disclose AI assistance honestly; the rule makes it visible, not forbidden, and an undisclosed assist is a false representation. And treat the artefact requirements as the real bar: a credential's value is the evidence behind it, which this group is what defines.
