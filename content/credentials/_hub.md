# The pillar that makes a claim of mastery checkable by a stranger

Credentials is the pillar that turns learning into something a third party can verify. It exists because a certificate is worthless if no one can check what it stands for, and most educational credentials are exactly that — an assertion with no evidence a stranger can inspect. Credentials inverts this: every mark on its ladder is defined by the evidence it demands, reviewed against a frozen rubric, and published to a verification surface where anyone can confirm it and, crucially, see when it has been revoked. Its five groups are the Ladder, Evidence & Proof, Review, Lifecycle, and Verification.

## Why this pillar exists

Two failures define the credential market. The first is inflation: a credential that everyone passes certifies nothing, so its issuer quietly raises pass rates to sell more of them. The second is opacity: a credential you cannot verify is indistinguishable from a forgery. Credentials is built against both. The ladder's lowest rung is deliberately not a credential at all — a completion marker that says only that you finished, making no claim about mastery — precisely so that the higher rungs can mean something. And every real credential resolves to a public record showing its evidence class, its decay interval, and its revocation status, so that verification is a lookup rather than an act of trust.

## The map — what lives inside Credentials

**The Ladder** runs from the completion marker (explicitly not a credential), through Series Practitioner, Professional Operator, and Master-Fellow, to MetaX Contributor. **Evidence & Proof** defines the proof ladder P0–P3, the submission pack, the dossier templates, the artefact requirements, and the AI-disclosure rule. **Review** is the human layer: the reviewer handbook, the frozen D1–D5 rubric, the conflict-of-interest rules, published reviewer statistics, and the appeals process under MX-016. **Lifecycle** governs a credential over time: decay and renewal, revocation grounds, the integrity report, and the registry spec that defines credentials.json. **Verification** is the public surface: verify a credential, how verification works, what it does not prove, and the revocation list.

## How a credential is earned and checked

A learner assembles a dossier — the artefacts a course produced — and submits it against the proof ladder. A reviewer, bound by conflict-of-interest rules and applying the frozen rubric, grades it. If it passes, a record is written to the public registry with an evidence class and a decay date. Anyone can then look the credential up by its identifier, see what it certifies and what it explicitly does not, and check whether it has since been revoked. The appeals process ensures a rejected candidate is heard by someone other than the reviewer who rejected them — a direct application of the License's remedy-preservation duty.

## The worked frame — what verification does not prove

The most honest page in this pillar is the one on verification's limits. A verified MetaX credential proves that, on a stated date, a named reviewer judged a specific dossier to meet a published rubric. It does not prove present competence — competence decays, which is why credentials carry a renewal interval — and it does not prove the holder is honest or safe to hire. Stating this plainly is not a weakness; it is what distinguishes a credential that means something narrow and true from one that implies something broad and false.

## Limits of this pillar

Credentials certifies evidence, not virtue. It cannot verify what it did not review, and it says so on every record. Its verification surface is a static record store, not a live identity system, so it proves the credential exists and its status, not that the person presenting it is the holder. And while the ladder is live, the higher rungs — Master-Fellow and Contributor — are necessarily rare, because their evidence demands are heavy by design.

## The falsifier for the pillar

The pillar's governing claim is that publicly verifiable, decaying, revocable credentials carry more information than conventional certificates. The refutation: if holders of MetaX credentials perform no more reliably in the competence the credential names than holders of unverified certificates, the extra machinery adds cost without signal and should be cut back to the rubric alone.

## What this pillar asks of you

If you hold a credential, read its decay date and renew before it lapses; a lapsed credential is not a revoked one, but verification will show it as expired. If you rely on a credential, read what it does not prove before you trust it to carry more than it claims. And under ML-2.3 Part C, treat the revocation list as load-bearing: a credential surface that hides its revocations is committing the concealment the License names.
