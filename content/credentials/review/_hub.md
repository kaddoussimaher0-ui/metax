# The human layer that judges evidence against a frozen standard

Review is the group where a human judges a learner's dossier — the layer that turns submitted evidence into a decision. It exists because assessment that requires judgement cannot be fully deterministic, and the estate handles that honestly: rather than smuggle judgement into an opaque score, it puts a named reviewer, bound by conflict-of-interest rules and applying a frozen rubric, in the loop, with an appeal available. This group holds the reviewer handbook, the frozen D1–D5 rubric, the conflict-of-interest rules, the published reviewer statistics, and the appeals process under MX-016. Its five pages cover exactly those.

## Why review is human, frozen, and appealable

Three properties make review trustworthy. It is **human** where judgement is required, because a machine scoring a novel artefact would be the opaque automated decision the License names in HX-06. It is **frozen** because the D1–D5 rubric cannot be quietly loosened, so the standard a learner studied against is the standard they are judged by. And it is **appealable** because a decision a learner cannot contest is the remedy foreclosure the License names in HX-18 — so an appeal goes to someone other than the original reviewer, and its outcome is logged.

## The map — what lives in this group

**Reviewer Handbook** states how a reviewer applies the rubric. **The Frozen Rubric (D1–D5)** is the five-dimension standard, frozen against drift. **Conflicts of Interest** states the rules that keep a reviewer independent of the learner. **Reviewer Statistics** publishes reviewers' aggregate decisions so calibration is visible. **Appeals (MX-016)** states how a decision is contested, decided by someone other than the party appealed against.

## The worked instance — appeals under MX-016

The appeals page is the group's clearest instance of the estate's remedy-preservation discipline. Under MX-016, a learner who disputes a decision is heard by a reviewer other than the one who made it, and the appeal outcome is logged. Crucially, the estate's own curator does not decide appeals against his own determinations — the independence runs all the way up. This is the License's interpretive-transparency and remedy-preservation duties applied to credentialing: a rejected learner is not stranded with the person who rejected them, and the appeal leaves a public record.

## Limits of this group

Review certifies what was submitted and reviewed, not the whole learner. Human judgement introduces variance, which the published reviewer statistics make visible rather than hide. And the frozen rubric constrains reviewers deliberately, which some will find rigid — that rigidity is the point, because a rubric that reviewers could bend would drift toward inflation.

## The falsifier for this group

The group's governing claim is that human, frozen-rubric, appealable review produces more trustworthy credentials than opaque or unappealable scoring. The refutation: if credentials issued through this review process prove no more reliable, and no more contestable, than credentials issued by an automated score, the human layer adds cost without benefit.

## What this group obliges

If you submit for a credential, study the frozen rubric — it is the exact standard you will be judged against, and it will not move. If you are rejected, use the appeal; it goes to an independent reviewer and is logged. And if you review, apply the rubric without loosening it and declare your conflicts, because the credential's value rests on the independence and the frozen standard this group protects.
