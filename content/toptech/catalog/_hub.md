# The inventory that renders from data rather than asserting counts

The Catalog is the live inventory of TopTech — every series, every course, the map that relates them, and the decay state of each — and its defining property is that it renders from dated data rather than asserting numbers in prose. It exists because a curriculum that claims "forty-seven series" in a sentence has already begun to lie the moment one is added or retired, while a catalog that renders its count from the routes data cannot. This group is where a learner sees the whole shape of TopTech at once, with each series carrying its status and its decay class. Its five pages are all series, all courses, the curriculum map, the decay state by series, and the catalog changelog.

## Why the catalog is data-driven

The estate's no-invented-numbers law is strictest here, because a catalog is nothing but numbers and relationships. Every count on these pages carries a source: the series count comes from the catalog data, the course count from the same, and the decay states from each series's own dated review. This means the catalog is always accurate to the data, and a reader who distrusts a count can follow its source attribute to the file that produced it. A curriculum that cannot be audited this way is a brochure, and the catalog refuses to be one.

## The map — what lives in this group

**All Series (S01–S47)** lists every series with its branch, tier, status, and decay class. **All Courses** lists the courses within live series. **Curriculum Map** shows how series relate — prerequisites, branches, and the paths through them. **Decay State by Series** shows, per series, how current its material is against its review interval. **Catalog Changelog** records every addition, retirement, and status change, dated.

## The worked instance — decay state as a first-class view

The decay-state page is the group's sharpest instance of the estate's method. Rather than present the catalog as a static list, it shows each series against its decay interval: a series whose review date has passed is marked stale on its own face, not silently trusted. This is the decay-awareness rail applied to the whole catalog at once — a learner can see, before investing a quarter in a series, whether its material is current or overdue for review. A catalog that hid decay would be selling potentially expired knowledge as fresh.

## Limits of this group

The catalog reflects the data, and on a demo that data is itself partly provisional — some series are planned rather than written, and their entries say so. The decay states are only as current as the last review. And the counts are accurate to the routes and catalog data, which is the source of truth; where prose elsewhere in the estate states a number, it should defer to the catalog, and a divergence is a defect.

## The falsifier for this group

The group's governing claim is that a data-driven, decay-aware catalog serves a learner better than an asserted list. The refutation: if learners using the catalog's decay states choose series no more wisely than learners shown a plain list, the extra machinery adds cost without benefit.

## What this group obliges

Read a series's decay state before you commit to it; a stale series is one whose material may have expired. Follow a count's source if you doubt it — the catalog is auditable by design. And treat the changelog as the record: every change to the catalog is dated there, so the catalog's history is as inspectable as its present.
